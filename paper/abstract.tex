We study a deliberately narrow spatial-inference task: locate a \emph{hidden} ball from
the configuration of the players alone, the way a spectator infers where play is headed.
On a de-biased benchmark of broadcast frames with the ball inpainted away ($n=260$), no
current frontier vision--language model (GPT-5.4, Llama-4-Maverick) reliably beats a
camera-center baseline on off-center balls---GPT-5.4 is at chance, Llama-4 below it. A
permutation-invariant network of $\sim$14k parameters ($\sim$60\,kB), trained only on
player positions and velocities, cracks the same off-center cases. We then open this
specialist. Across \textbf{12 matches from two leagues} (250k frames, leave-one-match-out),
ten interpretable geometric features fed to a gradient-boosted tree match the deep
network, and a single feature---the \emph{velocity-weighted centroid}---dominates.
Ablations show the signal is collective \emph{motion}: removing velocity costs
$0.035\pm0.002$ (12/12 matches), and the model's error is highest and its velocity
features useless precisely when the ball is \emph{still} (12/12). A cross-sport study
(soccer vs.\ basketball, 231k frames) locates the zero-shot transfer asymmetry in the
velocity channel: it collapses when velocities are removed. Finally, a mixture-density
head is globally calibrated yet systematically over-confident on loose balls in open
space---the same hard cases the VLMs fail. The capability VLMs lack is specific, cheap
to learn, and mechanistically simple: reading where the running mass of players is
heading.
