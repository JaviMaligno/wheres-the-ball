Frontier vision--language models cannot locate a ball they cannot see; a tiny model can.
We probe this with a deliberately narrow task: infer a \emph{hidden} ball's position from
the players alone, the way a spectator does. On a de-biased benchmark of broadcast frames
with the ball inpainted away ($n=260$), no current frontier VLM (GPT-5.4, Llama-4-Maverick)
reliably beats a camera-center baseline on off-center balls---GPT-5.4 is no better than
chance by win-rate, Llama-4 below it. A permutation-invariant network of $\sim$13k
parameters ($\sim$50\,kB), trained only on player positions and velocities, cracks the same
off-center cases. We then open this
specialist. Across \textbf{12 matches from two leagues} (250k frames, leave-one-match-out),
eleven interpretable geometric features fed to a gradient-boosted tree recover 91\% of a
tuned deep specialist's gain over the baseline, and a single feature---the
\emph{velocity-weighted centroid}---dominates.
Ablations show the signal is collective \emph{motion}: removing velocity costs
$0.035\pm0.002$ (12/12 matches), velocity's contribution collapses when the ball is
\emph{still} (12/12), and error is highest for the still ball (10/12). A cross-sport study
(soccer 250k + basketball 231k frames) locates the zero-shot transfer asymmetry in the
velocity channel: it collapses when velocities are removed. Finally, a mixture-density
head is globally calibrated yet systematically over-confident on loose balls in open
space---the same hard cases the VLMs fail. The capability VLMs lack is specific, cheap
to learn, and mechanistically simple: reading where the running mass of players is
heading.
